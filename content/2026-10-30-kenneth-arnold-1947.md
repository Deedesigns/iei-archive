## The afternoon that started it all

On **Tuesday, June 24, 1947**, a 32-year-old businessman named **Kenneth Arnold** was flying his small single-engine plane from Chehalis to Yakima, Washington. Arnold sold fire-control equipment around the Northwest and flew himself to his clients. On the way he took a detour around **Mount Rainier** to look for a Marine Corps transport plane that had crashed there months earlier, because a reward had been offered for finding it. (Even the size of the reward drifts between sources: the *East Oregonian* quoted him saying $10,000, while later accounts say $5,000.)

He told the *East Oregonian* he was cruising at about **9,200 feet** and about **110 miles per hour**. A little before 3 p.m., a bright flash lit up his plane. Looking for the source, he saw **nine shiny objects** strung out in a long chain near Rainier, flashing in the sun "like a mirror" as they flipped and weaved along the ridges.

What he did next is why this case still matters. He tried to rule out a trick of the light by rocking his plane and, by his account, rolling down his window, and the objects were still there. Then, believing he was looking at some new kind of jet, he **timed them** with the clock on his instrument panel as they crossed from Mount Rainier to **Mount Adams**: **1 minute 42 seconds**.

## What he actually said

Arnold talked to reporters in Pendleton, Oregon, the next day, and his early descriptions are preserved in the newspapers of June 25 to 28, 1947. They matter because later retellings, including some of his own, shifted.

- **Shape.** In the *East Oregonian* on June 26 he called the objects "flat like a pie pan and somewhat bat-shaped" and "saucer-like." An AP story the next day quoted him: "They were half-moon shaped, oval in front and convex in the rear." He said he could see **no tails**.
- **Motion.** They moved "like the tail of a Chinese kite," weaving and flipping, "like a fish flipping in the sun."
- **Size.** He compared them to a DC-4 airliner that was also in view, and said they were "slightly smaller than a DC4."
- **Distance.** He put them roughly **20 to 30 miles** away, and in 1950 told CBS's Edward R. Murrow "about 23 miles."
- **Doubt.** He admitted he "might have erred 200-300 miles" in figuring the speed, and said: "It seems impossible, but there it is."

**Where "flying saucer" came from.** In his 1950 Murrow interview, Arnold said the newspapers got him wrong: "I said that they flew like they take a saucer and throw it across the water... They said that I said that they were saucer-like; I said that they flew in a saucer-like fashion." The record is messier than that. Newspapers in the first days do quote him using "saucer-like," "pie pan," and "disk" for the *shape*. Either way, headline writers ran with it. By June 26, a Chicago headline read "Supersonic Flying Saucers Sighted by Idaho Pilot," and a new phrase entered the language.

## The speed problem

The heart of the case is a number. Speed is distance divided by time, and Arnold's time (1:42) was fixed by his clock. The distance was not. Different 1947 and later accounts give the Rainier-to-Adams stretch as 42, 47, or 48 to 50 miles, and each gives a different speed.

Arnold himself never trusted his top numbers. He calculated **about 1,700 mph**, and in a 1977 interview with the *Seattle Times* recalled "something like 1,760 miles an hour." But because he didn't know exactly where the objects faded from view, he publicly rounded down to **1,200 mph**, still nearly double the **623.7 mph** world record Col. Albert Boyd had set in a P-80R just five days earlier. (One United Press story from June 26 even claimed 42 miles in 1:42 was "about 1,205 mph." The arithmetic actually gives about 1,480 mph, a small reminder of how fast the numbers got garbled.)

This is where the whole debate lives. As Air Force UFO project chief **Edward Ruppelt** explained in his 1956 book, analysts in the late 1940s split into two camps:

- **"He saw jets."** Arnold said the objects were 45 to 50 feet long. Something that small would have been hard to see at 20-plus miles. So, this camp argued, they must have been **much closer** than he thought, and a closer object crossing the same angle in 102 seconds would be moving at **about 400 mph**, ordinary jet speed. Layers of warm and cold air could explain the shimmering "skipping."
- **"He knew where they were."** Arnold was an experienced mountain pilot who knew the Cascades well, and he said the objects passed **behind** a peak at one point, which would pin their distance. If that's right, the high speed stands, and the objects would have to be much **bigger** than he estimated (Ruppelt cites about 210 feet) to be visible at all.

The two camps are using the same facts. The disagreement is entirely about **how far away the objects were**, something no one could measure, because the only instrument in the case was one man's eyes.

## What the government concluded (it depends which file)

The official record does not speak with one voice:

- A July 1947 Army Air Forces counterintelligence interviewer, **Frank M. Brown**, wrote that "Mr. Arnold actually saw what he saw," adding that if he could write such a report without having seen anything, "he should be writing Buck Rogers fiction."
- Another July 1947 intelligence report, by **1st Lt. Hal L. Eustace**, found Arnold "reasonably well balanced, although excitable" and filed the episode among "silly-season episodes."
- The Air Materiel Command wrote that the report "cannot bear even superficial examination," and an Air Force document concluded that "the objects of this sighting were due to a mirage," according to the *Seattle Times*' reading of the declassified files.
- Yet Ruppelt, who ran Project Blue Book, wrote in 1956 that the case was "just another UFO report marked 'Unknown.'" He added the key caveat: "if Arnold actually did see the UFO's go *behind* a mountain peak, and if he knew his exact position at the time, the UFO problem cannot be lightly sloughed off; but there are always 'ifs' in UFO reports."

## Every explanation, and its weak spot

Researcher **Martin Kottmeyer** joked in 1997 that Arnold's must be "the most solved case in ufology," because more solutions have been offered for it than for any other. Here are the main ones, with the main problem each faces.

- **Ordinary jets or other aircraft, closer than he thought.** (Air Force analysts, Hartmann, Ridpath.) *Problem:* no pilots ever came forward, the formation was unconventional, Arnold saw no tails, and the pilot of the DC-4 in the area reported seeing nothing unusual.
- **Mirages or wave clouds over the mountains.** (The Air Force's "mirage" conclusion; Steuart Campbell's idea of mirages of distant peaks; meteorologist Richard Reed's orographic clouds.) *Problem:* these stay put, and Arnold described fast motion across a wide stretch of sky. Kottmeyer calls Campbell's version, which requires Arnold to mistake smaller peaks for Rainier and Adams, implausible.
- **Snow blowing off ridges.** (Donald Menzel, 1953.) *Problem:* Ruppelt says Air Force investigators called it "impossible" in June.
- **Water drops on the window.** (Menzel, 1977.) *Problem:* Arnold said he rolled the window down and still saw them.
- **A string of daytime meteor fireballs.** (Keay Davidson and Philip Klass, 1997.) Arnold first noticed a bright flash, saw no tails on his objects, and timed a long chain. *Problem:* Klass himself had to argue that Arnold's 1:42 timing was probably wrong, and fireballs don't usually weave along ridgelines for nearly two minutes.
- **A flock of large birds such as American white pelicans.** (James Easton and others.) Pelicans are big, pale underneath, can fly high, and look crescent-shaped. *Problem:* it requires the objects to be much closer than Arnold believed, so it shares the "jets" theory's distance argument. It is, however, consistent with one thing Arnold said: his very first thought was a flock of geese.
- **Extraterrestrial craft.** *Problem:* there is no physical evidence, no photo, no radar, and no independent witness who saw the same nine objects at the same time from a second position. Arnold himself, by July 1947, said that if they weren't the Army's he leaned toward another planet, and by 1950 he said he was "inclined to believe it's of an extra-terrestrial origin." That is an opinion, not evidence.

**Corroboration?** A few people in Washington reported flashes or discs that day, including a fire lookout who reported flashes over Rainier at about 3 p.m., according to later ufologist compilations. These reports were mostly gathered after Arnold's story was national news, and none gives an independent measurement of distance or speed. Within weeks, hundreds of "flying disc" reports flooded in nationwide, which shows how contagious the idea was more than it confirms what Arnold saw.

## Claims vs. evidence at a glance

- **Claim: Nine craft flew at 1,200 to 1,700 mph, impossible for 1947 technology.** *Evidence:* The speed follows only if the objects were as far away as Arnold estimated. With a closer distance, the same timing gives ordinary speeds. No measurement fixes the distance.
- **Claim: Arnold was a credible, careful witness.** *Evidence:* Strongly supported. Reporters, an Army interviewer, and Ruppelt all found him sincere, and he tried to test for reflections on the spot.
- **Claim: The government knew what they were.** *Evidence:* The files show the opposite: investigators disagreed among themselves, and the case ended up labeled both "mirage" and "Unknown."
- **Claim: He said the objects were saucers.** *Evidence:* Mixed. He later said "saucer" described the motion, but early newspapers quote him using "saucer-like" and "pie pan" for the shape.

## Our verdict: Unexplained

This case earns an honest **Unexplained**, and it is worth being precise about what that means. Kenneth Arnold almost certainly saw *something*, and none of the prosaic explanations fits every detail as he reported it. Even Martin Shough, a researcher who has written one of the most detailed analyses of the case, told the *Seattle Times* in 2017: "I am resigned to never knowing what Arnold saw."

But "unexplained" is not "alien." All of the extraordinary parts of the story, the speed and the size, come from one person's estimate of distance to small, fast-moving glints, made from a moving airplane with no instruments except a clock. Change that one estimate and the mystery shrinks toward jets, birds, or optical effects. The sighting's real legacy is cultural. It gave us the phrase "flying saucer," launched the 1947 wave, and led the Air Force to set up the projects that became Blue Book. As evidence for extraterrestrial visitors, it is a sincere report that can't be checked.
