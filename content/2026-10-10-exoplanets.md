## From zero to thousands

The first planets confirmed outside our solar system were found in 1992 orbiting a pulsar, and in 1995 Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz detected 51 Pegasi b around a Sun-like star, work that earned the 2019 Nobel Prize in Physics. As of October 8, 2026, the NASA Exoplanet Archive lists **6,445 confirmed exoplanets**, more than 1,000 of them found with data from NASA's TESS mission.

## How they are found

![Stacked bar chart of confirmed exoplanets per discovery year, coloured by method; transits dominate after 2014](exoplanets-by-year.png)
*Confirmed exoplanets by discovery year and method (6,445 planets; 2026 is a partial year). Chart: IEI. Data: [NASA Exoplanet Archive](https://exoplanetarchive.ipac.caltech.edu/), Planetary Systems table, queried October 13, 2026.*

Most known exoplanets were found by the **transit method**, which watches for a star dimming slightly as a planet passes in front of it. NASA's Kepler mission used this method to discover about 2,800 planets. The **radial velocity** method, which detects a star wobbling as a planet tugs on it, accounts for most of the rest.

## The habitable zone

Astronomers look for planets in a star's *habitable zone*, the range of distances where liquid water could exist on a rocky surface. Being in that zone does not mean a planet is habitable; it is only a starting filter. Famous examples include the seven Earth-sized planets of TRAPPIST-1, several of which orbit in or near that zone.

## Searching for biosignatures

The James Webb Space Telescope can analyse starlight passing through some planets' atmospheres, looking for gases such as water vapour, carbon dioxide and methane. A combination of gases that is hard to explain without biology would be a *biosignature*. In 2025 a team reported a tentative hint of dimethyl sulfide on the planet K2-18b, but other researchers found the signal was not statistically robust, and it remains unconfirmed.

## Where this leaves the question

- No exoplanet has confirmed evidence of life.
- Planets are common: most stars appear to host at least one.
- Next-generation telescopes, like NASA's proposed Habitable Worlds Observatory, are designed specifically to search Earth-like planets for biosignatures.
