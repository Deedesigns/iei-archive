## Seven worlds around a star you can't see

If you wanted to design the best possible place to look for air on another Earth, you might come up with something like **TRAPPIST-1**. It is an **ultracool red dwarf** about **40 light-years** (12 parsecs) away. The star has about **8% of the Sun's mass** and is only a little wider than Jupiter. It is cooler than about 2,700 K and invisible to the naked eye. Packed tightly around it are **seven rocky planets roughly the size of Earth**.

The story began with a small robotic telescope in Chile called **TRAPPIST** (the Transiting Planets and Planetesimals Small Telescope). In 2015 it caught three planets crossing in front of the star. In May 2016, Michaël Gillon and colleagues reported them in *Nature* as "temperate Earth-sized planets," and pointed out that stars like this make up roughly 15% of the objects near the Sun. In February 2017, a second *Nature* paper, combining ground telescopes with NASA's Spitzer Space Telescope, raised the count to **at least seven**.

Here is why astronomers got so excited, and still are:

- **The planets are huge compared with their star.** Each transit blocks a relatively large share of starlight, so the signal from any atmosphere is much bigger than it would be for an Earth around a Sun-like star.
- **The years are short.** All seven planets orbit closer to their star than Mercury is to the Sun, with years lasting from **1.5 to about 19 days**. Telescopes can watch many transits in a single season.
- **They are a family.** The six inner planets form a near-**resonant chain**: their orbital periods line up in near-whole-number ratios. Gillon's team read this as a sign that the planets formed farther out and migrated inward together. Their gentle tugs on one another also let astronomers measure the planets' **masses** from tiny shifts in transit timing.

By 2021, Eric Agol and colleagues had used those timing shifts to pin down radii and masses to within a few percent. All seven have densities consistent with rocky worlds. NASA counts **e, f, and g as being in the habitable zone**, where liquid water could exist on the surface if the conditions are right. In NASA's description, b, c, and d are too hot and h is too cold.

## The catch: a small star with a big temper

The same star that makes TRAPPIST-1 easy to study may also make its planets hard to live on. Red dwarfs are slow to settle down. Greene and colleagues (2023) summarized the problem: stars like TRAPPIST-1 throw out **frequent X-ray and ultraviolet flares**, and they spend **a billion years or more** in an overluminous youth before reaching their steady adult brightness. Models have long warned that this phase could drive **extreme water loss** from planets in what is now the habitable zone (Luger & Barnes 2015). Those same models raise the possibility of oxygen building up without any life involved, as water molecules are broken apart and the hydrogen escapes to space.

Webb has confirmed that the star is a difficult target. NASA's own explainer says TRAPPIST-1's activity is **"much more intense than originally predicted."** Two kinds of interference keep showing up:

- **Flares.** In the five JWST transits of planet f, **at least one stellar flare went off in every single visit.**
- **Starspots and bright patches (faculae).** These can stamp fake "atmospheric" features onto a transit spectrum. This is called the **transit light source effect**. For planet d, stellar contamination added spurious slopes of **500 to 1,000 parts per million**, far larger than any plausible atmospheric signal.

NASA says untangling the star from the planets may take **"potentially hundreds of transits."**

## What Webb has actually measured

JWST has two main ways to look for air on these planets:

- **Thermal emission.** Measure how much heat comes from a planet's dayside at 15 microns as it slips behind the star. A bare, dark rock gets very hot on the side facing the star. An atmosphere moves heat around to the nightside and lowers the dayside temperature. Carbon dioxide also absorbs strongly at 15 µm.
- **Transmission spectra.** During a transit, look for starlight filtered through an atmosphere, which shows up as wavelength-dependent dips.

Here is the planet-by-planet scorecard, using only published results.

## TRAPPIST-1 b: a hot, dark rock

- **The measurement (Greene et al. 2023, *Nature*).** Five MIRI eclipses detected b's heat at 15 µm at **8.7σ**. The dayside brightness temperature came out to **503 K** (about 230 °C). That nearly matches the **508 K** expected for a dark, bare rock that doesn't redistribute heat at all. The team concluded b has **"little or no planetary atmosphere,"** with no detectable CO₂.
- **The 12.8 µm follow-up (Ducrot et al. 2024).** Ten eclipses across two filters left two options: an airless planet with a **fresh, unweathered ultramafic surface**, which could hint at recent geological activity, or an exotic, thick, hazy CO₂ atmosphere with a temperature inversion. The authors said this second option comes "with some caveats."
- **The phase curve (Gillon, Ducrot et al. 2026, *Nature Astronomy*).** This settled the question. Webb followed b around its entire orbit and found a dayside of **490 ± 17 K**, **no significant nightside emission**, and no shift in the hottest spot, all pointing to a **dark, airless surface**.

## TRAPPIST-1 c: no Venus here, at least not a thick one

Planet c gets only about **8% more starlight than Venus**, so it was a natural test of whether a "Venus twin" could exist around a red dwarf.

- **Zieba et al. 2023 (*Nature*).** Four eclipses gave a dayside of **380 ± 31 K**. That ruled out cloud-free CO₂-rich atmospheres from about **10 bar down to 0.1 bar**, and disfavored a Venus-like atmosphere with sulfuric acid clouds at 2.6σ. The team calculated that c probably formed with **less than about 9.5 Earth oceans of water**, hinting at "a limited reservoir of volatiles" for the system's potentially habitable planets.
- **The 2026 phase curve.** It found a **369 ± 23 K** dayside and a cold nightside. This fits either an airless, somewhat reflective surface or a **thin, oxygen-rich atmosphere**. For both b and c, surface pressures **above about 1 bar are strongly disfavored**.

## TRAPPIST-1 d: not Earth, not Venus, not Titan

Planet d is the smallest of the inner three, about **0.8 Earth radii**. It orbits at 2% of the Earth–Sun distance with a four-day year, right at the inner edge of the habitable zone.

- **The observations.** Piaulet-Ghorayeb and colleagues (2025, *ApJ*) took two NIRSpec transits and corrected for the star's contamination. What was left was **flat to within about 100 to 150 ppm**.
- **What that rules out.** The spectrum excludes clear hydrogen-rich atmospheres. It also rejects, at more than 95% confidence, a haze-free Titan, a cloud-free Venus, early Mars, the Archean Earth, and a cloud-free modern Earth.
- **What's still possible.** As Piaulet-Ghorayeb put it, d "could have an extremely thin atmosphere that is difficult to detect, somewhat like Mars," or "very thick, high-altitude clouds," or "it could be a barren rock, with no atmosphere at all."

## TRAPPIST-1 e: the first real look at a habitable-zone world

In September 2025, the JWST DREAMS team published the first four NIRSpec transits of **TRAPPIST-1 e** (Glidden et al. 2025, *ApJ Letters*, with a companion paper by Espinoza et al.).

- **What's ruled out.** A **primordial hydrogen-helium atmosphere is unlikely**. A thick, **CO₂-dominated atmosphere is weakly disfavored** (about 2σ), so e does not look like a Venus or a Mars.
- **What still fits:** a **nitrogen-rich atmosphere** with traces of CO₂ or methane, more like Earth's, or a **bare rock**. The paper finds no strong evidence for or against an atmosphere; in NASA's summary, there is an **"equal chance"** one exists. A tentative hint of methane is, in the authors' words, "at best, a hint to be investigated further."
- **Why it matters.** If e does have air, "a little greenhouse effect goes a long way," as team member Nikole Lewis of Cornell University put it. The planet is thought to be **tidally locked**, with one side always facing the star. Even a thin atmosphere could keep a global ocean liquid, or an open-water patch beneath the star surrounded by ice.
- **What's next.** **Fifteen more transits** are being taken back-to-back with transits of planet b. Because b is airless, it can serve as a clean reference for subtracting the star's contamination.

## TRAPPIST-1 f: the star behaved, sort of

The newest result is **TRAPPIST-1 f**, from Lim and colleagues in the *Astronomical Journal* (accepted August 2026). They used five NIRISS transits.

- **The surprise.** Despite a flare in every visit, the team found **no evidence of the starspot contamination** that had plagued b, c, and d. They suggest this may be because f crosses a different latitude of the star's face.
- **What's ruled out.** The combined data reject hydrogen-helium atmospheres thicker than about **3 millibars** at 95% confidence. A 1-bar hydrogen atmosphere is rejected at 85σ.
- **What's left open.** The data **cannot rule out a thick (more than 1 bar) nitrogen-dominated atmosphere**. Limits on other heavy-molecule atmospheres changed depending on which data pipeline the team used.
- **A bonus.** Interior modeling suggests f could hold **a substantial amount of water**. The authors flag that result as dependent on assumptions about the planet's makeup.

## TRAPPIST-1 g and h: waiting in the archive

The Lim paper notes that **JWST has now observed all seven planets**. NASA's explainer likewise says Webb has collected data on every one. A NIRSpec study of g (Benneke et al.) is described as under review. Results for g and h are not yet published, so we're leaving them blank rather than guessing.

## Evidence, claims, and speculation

**What the evidence shows:**

- The two innermost planets, **b and c, have no thick atmospheres**. Planet b looks like bare rock.
- **Planet d has no Earth-like, Venus-like, or Titan-like atmosphere**, though it could have a very thin or very cloudy one.
- For the habitable-zone planets **e and f, only hydrogen-rich atmospheres have been ruled out**. Thinner, heavier, Earth-like nitrogen atmospheres are still allowed.
- The star is very active and is the **single biggest source of uncertainty** in all of this.

**What's claimed but unsettled:**

- Whether the outer planets kept their air. Researchers genuinely disagree. Some papers argue that airless inner planets do **not** rule out atmospheres farther out. If d is airless, the three inner planets formed with **4 or fewer Earth oceans** of water, which "would not preclude atmospheres on the cooler habitable-zone planets TRAPPIST-1 e, f, and g." Others argue that **no significant atmosphere** is likely to have survived on any of the seven. The split depends partly on how quickly each young planet's magma ocean solidified and locked volatiles away.

**What's speculation (for now):**

- **Oceans on e or f.** Whether e or f is an ocean world is still speculation. It is physically plausible if they have atmospheres, but it is untested.
- **Life.** No result from TRAPPIST-1 says anything about life, for or against.

## So what does this mean for life?

Some honest expectation-setting first. NASA's own explainer says **"Webb is not likely to detect biosignatures on these planets."** JWST's job here is a more basic one: finding out **whether rocky planets around the most common type of star in the galaxy can keep an atmosphere at all**. That matters a great deal. Red dwarfs vastly outnumber Sun-like stars. If their planets are routinely stripped bare, the galaxy's real estate for Earth-like life shrinks dramatically. If e or f turns out to have nitrogen air, red-dwarf planets become a prime place to look.

The results so far are pushing hard in one direction for the inner planets. For the planets that matter most, they are pushing in **no direction yet**. That is not a failure. These measurements are of starlight filtered through, or emitted by, Earth-sized worlds 40 light-years away, at the level of tens of parts per million. "It's incredible," said Ana Glidden of MIT, who led the study of possible atmospheres for planet e, "to measure the details of starlight around Earth-sized planets 40 light-years away and learn what it might be like there, if life could be possible there."

The next steps are already underway:

- the **15 extra transits of e**, paired with b as a stellar-contamination reference;
- longer looks at f;
- the first published results for g and h; and
- Webb's **Rocky Worlds** Director's Discretionary Time program, which NASA highlights as the next big push on rocky-planet atmospheres.

Beyond Webb, the European Southern Observatory's **Extremely Large Telescope** and NASA's proposed **Habitable Worlds Observatory** are the instruments that might someday take the step from "is there air?" to "is there life?"

**No verdict today.** This is an open scientific question, not a single claim to rule on. We'll update this page as the e, f, g, and h results come in.
