## The night of August 15, 1977

![Computer printout with the characters 6EQUJ5 circled in red and "Wow!" written in the margin](wow-printout.jpg)
*Jerry Ehman's annotated Big Ear printout. Credit: Big Ear Radio Observatory and North American AstroPhysical Observatory (NAAPO), via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Wow_signal.jpg), public domain.*

Ohio State University's **Big Ear** radio telescope was running a SETI survey when it recorded a strong, narrowband signal from the direction of the constellation Sagittarius. Days later, volunteer astronomer Jerry Ehman reviewed the printout, circled the sequence of characters "6EQUJ5" and wrote "Wow!" in the margin.

## Why it stood out

![Bar chart of the six intensity values 6, 14, 26, 30, 19, 5 rising and falling over about 72 seconds](wow-intensity.png)
*The "6EQUJ5" code decoded: in Big Ear's printout, digits 1 to 9 and letters A to Z (A = 10) give signal strength above background, one character per roughly 12-second sample. The rise and fall matches a fixed source drifting through the beam. Chart: IEI, from the printout values documented by the published printout (see [Wikipedia: Wow! signal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wow!_signal)).*

- The signal was close to the 1420 MHz hydrogen line, a frequency SETI researchers had long argued an intelligent civilization might use.
- It was narrowband, which natural sources rarely produce.
- Its rise and fall over about 72 seconds matched what a fixed point in the sky would look like as Earth's rotation carried it through the telescope's beam.

## Why it is not proof

The signal never repeated. Follow-up searches by Ehman, by Robert Gray and others, including with the Very Large Array, found nothing. A single unrepeated detection cannot rule out interference or an unusual natural event, and science requires repeat observations.

## Newer explanations

In 2024, a team led by Abel Méndez of the University of Puerto Rico at Arecibo proposed that the signal could have come from a cold hydrogen cloud briefly brightened by a strong burst from a source such as a magnetar. They also re-examined the archived data and suggested the signal was stronger than originally thought. The idea is a hypothesis, not a confirmed answer.

## Verdict

**Unexplained.** The Wow! signal remains SETI's best-known unrepeated candidate, with no confirmed natural or artificial origin.
