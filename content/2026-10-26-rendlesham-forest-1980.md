## Why this case matters

The **Rendlesham Forest incident** is the most famous UFO case in Britain, and it is often called "Britain's Roswell." What makes it stand out is who the witnesses were: not anonymous members of the public, but **U.S. Air Force security police and the deputy base commander** of two NATO airbases, RAF Bentwaters and RAF Woodbridge in Suffolk, England. The deputy commander, **Lt. Col. Charles Halt**, even wrote an official memo about it and recorded a tape in real time while he was in the forest.

That's a lot of documentation for a UFO case, which is great news, because it means we can check the story against what was written and recorded at the time, rather than against what people remembered decades later.

## The memo that started it all

![Typed U.S. Air Force memo on Department of the Air Force letterhead, dated 13 Jan 81, subject "Unexplained Lights," signed by Charles I. Halt, Lt Col, USAF, Deputy Base Commander](rendlesham-halt-memo.jpg)
*Lt. Col. Charles Halt's memo "Unexplained Lights," dated January 13, 1981, sent to the UK Ministry of Defence. Credit: Charles I. Halt, U.S. Air Force, via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Charles_Halt_memo.jpg), public domain (CC0).*

Halt's one-page memo, dated **January 13, 1981**, is the core document. In it he says:

- Around **3 a.m.**, two security patrolmen saw "unusual lights" outside the back gate of RAF Woodbridge. Thinking a plane had crashed, three patrolmen went into the forest on foot and reported "a strange glowing object" that was "metalic in appearance and triangular in shape," with a pulsing red light on top and blue lights underneath. As they approached, it "maneuvered through the trees and disappeared," and "the animals on a nearby farm went into a frenzy."
- The next day, **three depressions** "1 1/2" deep and 7" in diameter" were found where the object had supposedly been. The following night, radiation readings of **0.1 milliroentgens** were recorded, with peaks in the depressions.
- Later that night, "a red sun-like light" was seen through the trees. It "appeared to throw off glowing particles and then broke into five separate white objects." Then **three star-like objects** were seen low in the sky, about 10 degrees above the horizon, two to the north and one to the south. The one to the south "was visible for two or three hours and beamed down a stream of light from time to time."

Halt's memo dates the events to December 27 and 29, but Suffolk police records later showed the first night was actually the early morning of **December 26** (Boxing Day), and the second was the early morning of **December 28**. The memo reached the UK Ministry of Defence with a cover note dated January 15, 1981. It stayed out of public view until it was released by the U.S. government, and the *News of the World* splashed it across its front page in **October 1983**: "UFO LANDS IN SUFFOLK, And that's OFFICIAL."

## Night one: a fireball and a "beacon"

**What came down?** Ian Ridpath, a British astronomer and science writer who has investigated the case since 1983, found a key clue in the British Astronomical Association's Meteor Section newsletter of February 1981. It records a **brilliant fireball** seen over southern England on Boxing Day 1980 at **02:50 UT, plus or minus 5 minutes**. That's about 3 a.m. local time, the exact moment the patrol saw lights apparently dropping into the forest. Four witnesses rated it about as bright as a three-quarter Moon, lasting 3 to 4 seconds. Fireballs burn up very high in the atmosphere but often look like they've crashed nearby, and they make no sound. Another Bentwaters security guard, Richard Bertolino, said in a 2009 interview that he and his driver saw "a very bright falling star" with a blue-green glow that night, which looked like it was "falling between the two bases."

**What were they chasing?** The airmen went into the forest expecting to find a crashed aircraft, and they followed a light that kept moving ahead of them. Their own written statements, collected by Halt shortly afterward, are surprisingly clear about what it turned out to be. According to Ridpath, who quotes the statements:

- **John Burroughs** wrote: "Once we reached the farmer's house we could see a beacon going around so we went towards it. We followed it for about 2 miles before we could see it was coming from a lighthouse."
- **Ed Cabansag** wrote: "We got to a vantage point where we could determine that what we were chasing was only a beacon light off in the distance."

**What did the police see?** Suffolk police were called out shortly after 4 a.m. Their log, released under the UK Freedom of Information Act in 2005, says the only lights the officers could see were those from the **Orford Ness lighthouse**, several miles away on the coast. Police returned at 10:30 a.m. to look at the ground marks and thought they could have been made by an animal.

## Night two: Halt's tape

On the second night, Halt led a team back into the forest with a radiation meter, a night-vision "Starscope," and a micro-cassette recorder. The tape is the best evidence in the whole case, because it captures what the team said as it happened.

**The radiation.** On the tape, the readings at the site are described as "minor" and "three to four units," meaning about 0.03 to 0.04 milliroentgens per hour, with a brief spike of 0.07. Ridpath points out that the same levels were also picked up more than half a mile from the "landing site." The meter was an **AN/PDR-27**, a military survey meter built for measuring radiation after a nuclear accident, not for subtle background levels. According to Ridpath, the UK's National Radiological Protection Board contacted the manufacturer, who said Halt's peak reading of 0.1 was the "bottom reading on the lowest range" of the instrument and "of little or no significance." Checks of the site in 1982, 1987 and 2003 found nothing above background.

To be fair, not everyone at the time brushed this off. An internal Ministry of Defence note said 0.1 milliroentgens "seems significantly higher than the average background of about 0.015." The problem is that the instrument couldn't reliably measure levels that low in the first place.

**The flashing light.** The team saw a flashing light to the east, which Halt described on tape as being "like a pupil of an eye looking at you, winking." Several clues point to the lighthouse:

- On the tape, an airman calls out "There it is again... there it is." The interval between the calls is **5 seconds**, which was the flash rate of Orford Ness.
- The team's compass bearing to the light was about **110 degrees**. Ridpath calculates the true bearing to the lighthouse from that spot was **99 degrees**, a close match for a rough bearing taken at night by people on the move.
- Later on the tape, Halt says the light "looks like it's clear off to the coast. It's right on the horizon."
- In 1980, the lighthouse was among the brightest in England, at about **5 million candelas**, and it stood 28 meters above high water. From the forest's edge, it was visible only through a gap in distant trees. Step a few paces away and it vanished, which helps explain why it seemed to come and go.

**The star-like objects.** Halt's three low, colorful, star-like lights that "hovered" for hours fit bright stars near the horizon. Ridpath identifies the bright one to the south as **Sirius**, the brightest star in the night sky, which was setting at about 7 degrees above the horizon at 4 a.m. He identifies the two to the north as **Deneb and Vega**. Stars that low twinkle in different colors, seem to drift because of the eye's natural jitter, and appear to brighten and dim as thin clouds pass. Base commander **Col. Ted Conrad**, who was listening on the radio, went outside at Woodbridge with others and told researcher David Clarke in 2010 that they saw none of the lights Halt described.

**The radar.** Halt asked the command post to check with Eastern Radar, which reported seeing nothing. Simon Weeden, the Ministry of Defence official who handled Halt's memo, told Clarke in 2020 that RAF radar stations checked their logs and found nothing unusual over the holidays, so the ministry decided the report was "of no defence significance."

## The ground marks

The "landing marks" were three small depressions in a rough triangle, plus marks on nearby trees. Local forester **Vince Thurkettle**, who visited the site soon after, told the BBC in 2020 it was "an absolutely normal glade in the forest with three rabbit scrapes." He said the "burn marks" on the trees were cuts made by a forest ranger "with an axe."

## How the story grew

The most sensational claims in the Rendlesham story did not appear in the 1980 to 1981 records. They came later.

- **Jim Penniston's "craft."** Sgt. Jim Penniston later said he spent about 45 minutes examining and even touching a landed triangular craft, and recorded it in a notebook with sketches of symbols on its side. But according to Ridpath, Penniston's original witness statement says he got no closer than about 50 meters. His notebook bears a date and time that don't match the incident. Burroughs, who was with him, wrote to Ridpath in 2008 that Penniston "did not have time to make any sketches in a note book while this was going on." After hypnosis in 1994, Penniston came to believe the craft carried time travelers from humanity's future. In 2010, he added that the craft had telepathically given him pages of binary code. No other witness reports seeing a craft that night.
- **Halt's later account.** In a sworn affidavit in **June 2010**, Halt said he believed the objects "were extraterrestrial in origin" and that U.S. and UK security services had used "disinformation" to play the event down. He also wrote that the light was "dripping something that looked like molten metal," and that the lighthouse was "35 to 40-degrees off" from the unknown light. Neither the molten-metal detail nor the "exploding" light appears on his 1980 tape. Ridpath argues that Halt was used to seeing the lighthouse in the southeast from his quarters at Bentwaters, two miles north, and so didn't recognize it when it lay almost due east from the forest.
- **Beams on the nuclear storage area.** Halt later said beams came down into the Weapons Storage Area at Bentwaters. Tim Egercic, a security policeman on duty in that area that night, told author Robert Hastings: "I never saw or heard about a UFO at the WSA, or beams of light, or anything like that."
- **Other theories.** A former security policeman told the BBC in 2003 that he had pranked colleagues with modified vehicle lights, but there is no evidence it happened on these nights. A crashed Soviet satellite has also been suggested, without supporting evidence. In 2020, Burroughs published a book arguing the event involved experiments with an "energy field." He also told the BBC: "I never went on the record to say it was [a spaceship] because I didn't know."

## Claims vs. evidence at a glance

- **Claim: A craft landed in the forest.** *Evidence:* A fireball was seen at the same time. Airmen's own statements say they ended up following a lighthouse beacon, and police saw only the lighthouse. Penniston's close-up account surfaced years later and conflicts with his own original statement and with Burroughs.
- **Claim: Radiation proved something landed.** *Evidence:* Readings on Halt's tape were at background levels, and similar readings appeared half a mile away. The meter wasn't designed to measure background radiation, and later site checks found nothing.
- **Claim: The flashing light can't have been the lighthouse.** *Evidence:* The light flashed every 5 seconds, its bearing matched to within about 11 degrees, and Halt described it as "right on the horizon" toward the coast.
- **Claim: UFOs hovered over the bases for hours.** *Evidence:* The positions and behavior match setting bright stars. The base commander looked and saw nothing, and radar recorded nothing.
- **Claim: A cover-up.** *Evidence:* The Ministry of Defence's handling looks more like disinterest than concealment. It checked radar, found no threat, and filed it. Its file was released in 2001.
- **Open point:** David Clarke, who obtained the ministry's file, told the BBC in 2020 that what the three airmen experienced on the first night still has "an element of mystery." Witness memories have also changed a great deal over 40 years.

## Our verdict: Explained

Rendlesham is a fascinating case, and the witnesses were trained military personnel who were clearly rattled by what they experienced. But when you put the **1980 to 1981 records**, including the airmen's statements, the police log and Halt's own tape, next to the later retellings, the mystery mostly melts away. A **fireball** made it look as if something had crashed. Men searching a dark, unfamiliar forest for a crashed aircraft chased one of the **brightest lighthouses in England** as it flashed through a gap in the trees. **Rabbit diggings** became landing marks, **background radiation** on the wrong instrument became contamination, and **setting stars** became hovering craft. As skeptic Brian Dunning put it, "Separate pieces of poor evidence don't aggregate together into a single piece of good evidence."

The honest caveat is that no single explanation is proven for every detail of the first night, and the most dramatic testimony comes from people who were there. But the evidence recorded at the time doesn't point to anything extraterrestrial.
