## A question over lunch

![Black-and-white portrait of physicist Enrico Fermi in a tweed suit and striped tie, standing in front of a laboratory control panel with dials and switches](fermi-portrait.jpg)
*Enrico Fermi, photographed sometime between 1943 and 1949. Credit: U.S. Department of Energy, Office of Public Affairs (National Archives 558578), via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Enrico_Fermi_1943-49.jpg), public domain.*

The most famous question in the search for alien life wasn't asked at a conference or in a paper. It came up at lunch. Enrico Fermi, the Nobel Prize-winning physicist who built the first nuclear reactor, never published anything about it. For decades it was mostly a "legend of science," retold with different details each time.

In 1984 Eric M. Jones, a scientist at Los Alamos National Laboratory, set out to pin the story down. He wrote to the three surviving people at the table and published their letters in a short 1985 report, **"Where Is Everybody?": An Account of Fermi's Question** (LA-10311-MS). It's the key primary source, and it's well worth reading for yourself.

Here is what the eyewitnesses said:

- **The walk to lunch.** Emil Konopinski remembered that he, Fermi, Edward Teller and Herbert York were walking to lunch at Fuller Lodge, talking about flying saucers. He brought up a *New Yorker* cartoon blaming the city's missing public trash cans on "little green men." Fermi joked that it was "a very reasonable theory since it accounted for two separate phenomena." Teller recalled Fermi asking him how likely it was that within ten years we'd see "clear evidence of a material object moving faster than light." Teller said one in a million; Fermi said more like ten percent.
- **The question.** At the table, the talk had moved on. Konopinski: "Fermi surprised us with the question 'but where is everybody?' It was his way of putting it that drew laughs from us." York remembered it as "Don't you ever wonder where everybody is?" Teller wrote that "everybody around the table seemed to understand at once that he was talking about extraterrestrial life."
- **The follow-up.** York believed Fermi then ran through "a series of calculations on the probability of earthlike planets, the probability of life given an earth, the probability of humans given life, the likely rise and duration of high technology." He concluded "that we ought to have been visited long ago and many times over." The reason might be that interstellar flight is impossible, or "always judged to be not worth the effort," or that "technological civilization doesn't last long enough." York admitted he was "much hazier" about this part and told Jones he should "probably discount" it.
- **The date.** The memories disagreed. Teller thought it was "probably before 1950," and York guessed 1951. Jones traced the trash-can cartoon by Alan Dunn to the **May 20, 1950** issue of *The New Yorker*. After seeing it, Konopinski wrote that he was "now certain" the lunch took place in the **summer of 1950**.

One fun detail: in his letter, Teller corrected the popular version of the story. Fermi did **not** say "If you are right, then where is everybody?" According to Teller, the question came "from the clear blue."

## From a quip to an argument: Hart and Tipler

Fermi asked a question. The hard-edged *argument* came later.

In **1975**, astronomer Michael H. Hart published "An Explanation for the Absence of Extraterrestrials on Earth" in the *Quarterly Journal of the Royal Astronomical Society*. He called the observation that "There are no intelligent beings from outer space on Earth now" **"Fact A."** His reasoning: any civilization capable of space travel would eventually spread across the galaxy (Jones summarizes Hart's estimate as "a few million years"), which is a blink compared with the galaxy's age. So, Hart argued, "they are not here; therefore they do not exist." He then tried to rule out physical, sociological and timing explanations one by one.

In **1980**, mathematical physicist Frank Tipler went further with a paper bluntly titled "Extraterrestrial Intelligent Beings do not Exist." His key idea was self-replicating robot probes. A civilization able to talk across interstellar space would also build such machines, which could explore or colonize the galaxy "in less than 300 million years." Since no probes are in our solar system, Tipler concluded that "we are the only intelligent species now existing in this Galaxy."

That's why astronomer Robert H. Gray argued in *Astrobiology* (2015) that "The Fermi Paradox is Neither Fermi's Nor a Paradox." In his reading, Fermi was mostly wondering whether interstellar travel is feasible. The "they don't exist" conclusion is Hart's, extended by Tipler. Gray also notes the argument was once cited in the U.S. Congress as a reason to cancel NASA's SETI program.

## The Drake equation: putting numbers on the question

In **November 1961**, about a year after his pioneering Project Ozma radio search, Frank Drake hosted roughly a dozen scientists at the Green Bank observatory in West Virginia. Among them were Carl Sagan, Philip Morrison, Joshua Lederberg and Melvin Calvin. To set the agenda, Drake wrote down a formula, now the famous **Drake equation**:

**N = R\* × fp × ne × fl × fi × fc × L**

It multiplies the rate of star formation by the fraction of stars with planets, the number of habitable planets per system, and the fractions that develop life, intelligence and detectable technology. Then it multiplies by **L**, how long such civilizations keep sending detectable signals. The result, **N**, is the number of detectable civilizations in the Milky Way right now. York's memory of Fermi's back-of-the-envelope reasoning sounds a lot like this chain of probabilities, and Jones points out that resemblance in his report.

According to the SETI Institute, the 1961 attendees guessed about one new transmitting civilization appears per year, so N roughly equals L. Drake himself has suggested **N = 10,000**. Published estimates range from 1 to several million. Thanks to exoplanet surveys (see our page on [exoplanets and life](exoplanets-and-life.html)), we now have decent numbers for the planet terms. The biology terms (fl, fi) and the lifetime L are still almost pure guesswork.

## The big proposed answers

Dozens of solutions have been proposed. Here are the major families, labeled for what they are: **hypotheses**, not findings.

- **The Great Filter (Robin Hanson, 1998).** Hanson's essay "The Great Filter – Are We Almost Past It?" argues that because "space near us seems dead," there must be at least one extremely improbable step between dead matter and a civilization that spreads across space. The worrying part is that we don't know whether that step is behind us (the origin of life, complex cells, intelligence) or ahead of us (self-destruction). Hanson even argues that finding independent alien life would be "likely bad (though valuable) news," because it would suggest the early steps are easy and the filter lies ahead.
- **Rare Earth (Peter Ward & Donald Brownlee, 2000).** In their book *Rare Earth: Why Complex Life Is Uncommon in the Universe*, a paleontologist and an astronomer argue that microbes may be common, but animal-like complex life needs an unusual combination of conditions. If so, there may be very few civilizations to see.
- **The zoo hypothesis (John Ball, 1973).** In a short *Icarus* paper, Ball proposed that advanced civilizations may exist but deliberately leave us alone, like a wildlife preserve. The catch, as Hanson and others point out, is that it would have to be enforced *almost perfectly* by *everyone*. A single defector would break it.
- **Distance and timing.** Space is enormous and civilizations might be short-lived or spread far apart in time. Even at the original lunch, Teller remembered someone pointing out that we live "in the sticks, far removed from the metropolitan area of the galactic center." Hart's 1975 paper argued against "temporal explanations," but whether they fail depends on assumptions about how fast and how persistently anyone would actually expand.
- **We've barely looked.** This is the most down-to-earth answer, and it has real numbers behind it. In **2018**, Jason Wright, Shubham Kanodia and Emily Lubar built an eight-dimensional model of the "cosmic haystack" that radio SETI has to search: distance, sky direction, frequency, signal strength, bandwidth, repetition, polarization and more. They estimated that all the major radio searches so far cover a fraction of that haystack comparable to **about 8,000 liters of water out of all of Earth's oceans**, "somewhere between the volumes of a large hot tub and a small swimming pool." Earlier, Jill Tarter and colleagues had compared it to a drinking glass. The authors stress that the number depends heavily on where the haystack boundaries are drawn. Either way, the point stands: no detection yet is *not* the same as "nobody's there." Our pages on the [Wow! signal](wow-signal.html) and [BLC1](blc1-proxima-centauri-signal.html) show how hard even the best candidates are to pin down.

## "Dissolving" the paradox?

In **2018**, Anders Sandberg, Eric Drexler and Toby Ord of Oxford's Future of Humanity Institute posted a widely discussed paper called **"Dissolving the Fermi Paradox."** Their key point is a statistical one. Plugging single "best guess" numbers into the Drake equation hides how uncertain those numbers really are. Some factors, especially the chance that life starts on a habitable planet (fl), are uncertain by dozens of orders of magnitude.

When they replaced each point estimate with a realistic range of uncertainty, the picture changed:

- When they sampled from published estimates, about **64%** of raw literature estimates gave N > 100. Once the uncertainty was included, there was still about a **30%** chance that N < 1, meaning we'd be alone in our galaxy.
- With their own uncertainty ranges, the **median** N was only **0.32** (while the mean was 27 million). That gave a **52%** chance we're alone in the Milky Way and about **38%** that we're alone in the whole observable universe.
- After factoring in the "Fermi observation" (no signals, no visible galactic colonization), those numbers rose to **53%–99.6%** (alone in the galaxy) and **39%–85%** (alone in the observable universe), depending on which evidence is used.

Their conclusion is that an empty-looking sky "should not surprise us," so we don't need exotic explanations like universal self-destruction or a galactic zoo. They are careful to add that this "does not mean that we are alone." It's "a statement about our state of knowledge, rather than a new measurement." Critics note that the result depends heavily on how you model the uncertainty about life's origin. The authors themselves say their conclusion "can be driven by our claimed uncertainty in fl alone."

## Evidence, claims and speculation

- **Evidence (solid):** Fermi really did ask the question, at lunch at Los Alamos, most likely in summer 1950. Three eyewitnesses wrote it down. We have found thousands of exoplanets, and planets are common. No confirmed signal, probe or artifact from another civilization has been found.
- **Evidence (but limited):** Our searches have covered only a tiny slice of the possible ways to detect a civilization.
- **Claims (argued, not proven):** Hart and Tipler's argument that the absence of visitors means nobody exists. Sandberg, Drexler and Ord's argument that uncertainty alone explains the silence.
- **Speculation:** The Great Filter's location, the zoo hypothesis, and Rare Earth's claim that complex life is rare. These are thoughtful ideas, but none has direct evidence for or against it yet.

## Evidence verdict

There's no single claim to rule on here, so we're not giving this page a badge. Our honest assessment is that **the question is still open, and that's exciting.** The "paradox" rests on two big assumptions. The first is that the probability of life and intelligence is not tiny. The second is that civilizations inevitably spread out and become visible. Neither one has been measured. Today we can't tell whether the silence means *nobody's there*, *they're there but quiet or far away*, or simply *we haven't looked hard enough*.

The good news is that each of those can be tested. Finding life on [Europa](europa-clipper-ocean-moon.html), [Enceladus](enceladus-plume-organics.html) or Mars, or a solid biosignature on an exoplanet, would tell us whether life's first steps are easy. Bigger and deeper technosignature surveys keep shrinking the "we haven't looked" gap. Fermi's lunchtime joke has turned into a research program, and the answer will come from data, not from arguing.
