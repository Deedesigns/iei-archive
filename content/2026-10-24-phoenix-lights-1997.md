## A night when everyone was looking up

On the evening of **March 13, 1997**, a lot of people in Arizona were outside on purpose. **Comet Hale-Bopp**, the brightest comet in about 20 years, was near its closest approach to Earth and hung in the northwestern sky after sunset. Skeptic Robert Sheaffer argues that this is a big reason the night produced so many witnesses: people went out to see the comet and saw something else.

![Comet Hale-Bopp with its blue ion tail and white dust tail, next to the band of the Milky Way in a dark sky full of stars](phoenix-lights-hale-bopp.jpg)
*Comet Hale-Bopp and the Milky Way, photographed March 10, 1997, three days before the Phoenix Lights. Credit: ESO/E. Slawik, via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Comet_Hale-Bopp_Phot-mar14-hbs-1-hires.jpg), CC BY 4.0.*

What people saw that night became "the Phoenix Lights," often called the most widely witnessed UFO event in history. The most important fact about the case is one that took months to sink in: **there were two separate events**, about 90 minutes apart, with different explanations. Many later accounts, including some by the state's own governor, blend them into one.

## Event one: the V over Arizona (about 8:15 to 8:45 p.m.)

Reports of a V or "boomerang" of five lights moving south came from **Prescott at about 8:15 p.m.**, **Phoenix at about 8:30**, and **south of Tucson by about 8:45**. *Phoenix New Times* reporter Tony Ortega pointed out that covering roughly **200 miles in 30 minutes** works out to about **400 miles per hour**. That is airliner speed, not hovering.

Witnesses disagreed sharply on what they saw. Some described a single solid craft "the size of multiple football fields" that blotted out the stars. Others, including one of the two *New Times* writers who saw it, said the lights did not look connected. Ufologist Peter Davenport noted that reports gave different numbers of lights, different colors, and different speeds.

Two pieces of evidence carry the most weight:

- **The video.** Terry Proctor filmed the V for several minutes. Even with enhancement, nothing joins the lights, and the pattern **changes shape over a few seconds**. The lights move relative to one another, which means they were separate objects, not points on one rigid body.
- **The telescope.** Amateur astronomer **Mitch Stanley** in Scottsdale aimed his 10-inch Dobsonian telescope, magnifying about 60 times, at the leading lights. Under magnification, each light split into a pair on the tips of squarish wings. When his mother asked what he saw, he said "Planes." He told *New Times*: "They were planes. There's no way I could have mistaken that."

The "it blocked out the stars" reports have a simple optical explanation. Bright lights outlining a dark triangle make faint stars inside it hard to see, the same trick stage magicians call "black art."

**Whose planes were they?** This is the one genuinely loose end. Sheaffer (2016) and astronomer and retired Air Force pilot James McGaha (2015) both say the V was **five A-10s from Operation Snowbird**, an Air National Guard winter training program at Davis-Monthan Air Force Base in Tucson. In their account, the jets were returning from Nellis Air Force Base near Las Vegas along the standard air corridor, under visual flight rules, showing steady formation lights instead of blinking anti-collision lights. They landed at Davis-Monthan around 8:45 p.m. But as of *New Times'* March 1998 report, **no base had come forward to claim that flight**, and a Maryland Air National Guard spokesman said its A-10s never went north of Phoenix. So the "planes" part rests on direct observation, while the specific unit is a well-argued reconstruction, not a confirmed record.

**What about radar?** Claims that the V was "invisible to radar" don't hold up well. A Sky Harbor air traffic controller on duty that night told *New Times* that high-altitude traffic over Phoenix was tracked by the FAA center in Albuquerque, not the airport. A formation would also have shown as a single blip, which nobody would notice. The FAA said nobody asked for the radar tapes before they were routinely recycled (the deadline was March 28), so that data is gone.

## Event two: the hovering row of lights (about 10:00 p.m.)

At about **10 p.m.**, up to **nine brilliant lights** appeared in a row southwest of Phoenix. They seemed to hover for several minutes and then winked out one by one. This was the event that people filmed. Mike Krzyston's backyard video of all nine lights became the most famous footage of the case.

The explanation came out over the summer:

- In **June 1997**, KPNX-TV reporter Blair Meeks filmed military planes dropping flares over the gunnery ranges southwest of Phoenix. The footage looked strikingly like the March 13 lights.
- Within days, the *Tucson Weekly* reported that the **Maryland Air National Guard** had A-10s over the range that night and had dropped flares. Arizona National Guard spokesperson **Capt. Eileen Bienz** determined the flares were released at **10 p.m. over the North Tac range, about 30 miles southwest of Phoenix, at an unusually high 15,000 feet**. Davis-Monthan's spokesman had first said the base had no planes up. He later explained he had only been asked about the base's own aircraft, not visiting Guard units.
- McGaha says he spoke with the flight lead, **Ed Jones**, who confirmed the drop. Jones said the remaining flares were released at high altitude before the jets left the range. Jones confirmed it again to *The Arizona Republic* in 2007.

The flares were **LUU-2-series parachute illumination flares**, which are designed to light up the ground at night. McGaha and Nickell give their brightness as about **1.8 million candlepower** with a burn time of about **four minutes**. That is easily bright enough to be seen from Phoenix. Hanging under parachutes, they drift slowly, which reads as "hovering" from 30 to 70 miles away.

**Why did they vanish one by one?** For the Discovery Channel, image-processing expert **Leonid Rudin** of Cognitech lined up Krzyston's night video with a daytime shot from the same yard, where the **Sierra Estrella** mountains are visible. The lights sit just above the ridge and go out as they sink behind it, exactly as distant falling flares should. ASU astronomer **Paul Scowen** independently repeated the match for *New Times* and got the same answer.

## The "spectral analysis" claim

The best-known argument that the 10 p.m. lights were *not* flares came from **Jim Dilettoso** of Village Labs in Tempe. He said his computer analysis of the videotapes showed the lights were unlike any human-made light. *New Times* took his method to astronomers and to the maker of the software he used. They explained that he was only plotting the brightness of pixels along a line through a blurry blob on videotape, not measuring a spectrum. Camcorder footage of a distant point of light can't be analyzed that way. Dilettoso also admitted that two known flares in the same frame produced different graphs. An ASU astronomer compared the idea to "testing a portrait of Abraham Lincoln for his DNA."

## The governor's story

In **1997**, a few months after the sightings, Arizona Governor **Fife Symington** held a press conference at which his chief of staff appeared in an alien costume. In a **2007 CNN essay**, Symington wrote that he had himself seen "a massive delta-shaped craft silently navigate over Squaw Peak" that night. He called the Air Force's flare explanation "silly" because "flares don't fly in formation."

His testimony matters because he is a pilot and former Air Force officer, but it also shows how the two events get blended. Nobody says the flares flew in formation. The flares explain the 10 p.m. row of lights, and airplanes explain the moving V. Symington's essay itself allows that "there might very well have been military flares in the sky that evening."

## Other explanations and later copycats

- **A secret airship?** Some skeptics have suggested the V was a large experimental lighter-than-air craft, pointing to V-shaped airships built by JP Aerospace. The company's founder, John Powell, told *The War Zone* in 2020 that his company and its vehicles were "absolutely" not involved. That idea is not supported by any evidence, and Stanley's telescope view of wings doesn't fit it.
- **Repeat performances.** In March 2000, the Guard announced flare drops in advance to avoid a repeat panic. In February 2007, flares again lit up the Phoenix sky. In April 2008, four red "Phoenix Lights" turned out to be road flares tied to helium balloons. A neighbor watched the man launch them.

## Claims vs. evidence at a glance

- **Claim: A mile-wide solid craft passed silently over Phoenix.** *Evidence:* The only video shows five lights moving relative to one another. A telescope observer saw airplane wings. The timing implies about 400 mph along a standard air route.
- **Claim: The 10 p.m. lights hovered and could not have been flares.** *Evidence:* A Guard flare drop at 10 p.m. at 15,000 feet over a range southwest of Phoenix is confirmed by the Guard and by the flight lead. Two independent video analyses show the lights setting behind the Sierra Estrella.
- **Claim: Spectral analysis proved the lights were unknown.** *Evidence:* The method can't do what was claimed, according to astronomers and the software maker.
- **Claim: The military covered it up.** *Evidence:* The early denial from Davis-Monthan was real and fed suspicion, but it covered only the base's own planes. The Guard unit was identified within four months.
- **Open point:** No military unit has officially claimed the 8:30 p.m. formation, and FAA radar data was never saved.

## Our verdict: Explained

The Phoenix Lights were real lights seen by real people, and the reports were honest. They just weren't one object, and they weren't alien. The 10 p.m. display, which produced nearly all the photos and video, was a **documented high-altitude flare drop** by Maryland Air National Guard A-10s. The 8:30 p.m. V was **a formation of aircraft**, based on the shape-shifting video, a telescope view of wings, and a speed and route that match air traffic. One honest caveat is that the exact flight behind the V is a strong reconstruction, not an official record. That leaves a small gap in the paperwork, but no evidence of anything extraterrestrial.
