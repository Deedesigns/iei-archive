## Why Europa

Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech/SETI Institute, via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:PIA19048_realistic_color_Europa_mosaic_edited.jpg), public domain.*

Europa, one of Jupiter's four large moons, is covered by a shell of water ice criss-crossed with cracks, ridges and jumbled "chaos terrain". Beneath that shell, scientists think, lies a global saltwater ocean. NASA estimates it at 60 to 150 km (40 to 100 miles) deep, which would mean more than twice as much water as all of Earth's oceans combined. Liquid water, the right chemical ingredients and a source of energy are the three things astrobiologists look for when they ask whether a place could be *habitable*. That puts Europa near the top of the list of places in our solar system where life beyond Earth could exist today.

## What we actually know

- **The ocean (strong but indirect evidence).** Galileo's magnetometer detected a magnetic field around Europa that changes as the moon moves through Jupiter's own field. That is what you would expect if a layer that conducts electricity, most plausibly salty liquid water, lies under the ice. A key 2000 flyby, reported by Margaret Kivelson's team in *Science*, favoured a changing, induced field over a permanent one and strengthened the ocean case. The authors noted that the conducting layer need not be water, but water is the most likely candidate.
- **The ice shell.** Estimates of its thickness have ranged from about 3 km to more than 30 km. In 2025, data from NASA's Juno spacecraft, gathered during a September 2022 flyby, suggested the cold, rigid outer layer of the shell averages about **29 ± 10 km** thick in the region observed. That assumes pure water ice; a warmer layer beneath could make the total shell thicker. The same study found cracks and pores reaching only a few hundred metres down, too shallow on their own to carry nutrients between surface and ocean.
- **Carbon from inside.** In 2023 two teams using the James Webb Space Telescope, led by Samantha Trumbo and Gerónimo Villanueva, found carbon dioxide ice concentrated in Tara Regio, a young region of chaos terrain where ocean-derived salt had already been seen. CO2 is not stable on Europa's surface for long, so the teams concluded it came from an internal source, probably the ocean, and arrived geologically recently. Trumbo and Brown noted they could not rule out that it formed at the surface from ocean-derived material.
- **Oxygen from the surface.** Juno also measured how fast radiation splits Europa's surface ice into hydrogen and oxygen: about **12 kg of oxygen per second**, according to a 2024 *Nature Astronomy* paper. That is near the low end of earlier estimates. How much of that oxygen ever reaches the ocean, where it could feed chemistry useful to life, is unknown.

## The plume question

If water vapour vents from Europa into space, a spacecraft could sample the ocean without drilling. The evidence for that has weakened.

- **2014:** Lorenz Roth and colleagues reported ultraviolet emission from hydrogen and oxygen above Europa's south pole in Hubble images from 2012, and interpreted it as transient water-vapour plumes.
- **2018:** Xianzhe Jia's team re-examined Galileo's closest Europa flyby (below 400 km altitude, in 1997). They found a magnetic and plasma-wave signature consistent with the spacecraft passing through a plume.
- **2026:** A new reanalysis led by Roth, published in *Astronomy & Astrophysics*, examined Hubble images from 1999 and 2012 to 2020. It found **no localized emission in any of them**, including the 2012 image. The team traced the earlier signal mainly to an error in where Europa's disk was placed on the detector, and to a faint hydrogen haze around the whole moon that was not known in 2014. The Galileo result still stands but depends on modelling, and some researchers have questioned it.

Bottom line: Europa plumes are possible but not confirmed.

## The mission

Europa Clipper launched on a SpaceX Falcon Heavy from Kennedy Space Center on **October 14, 2024**, two years ago today. It is NASA's first mission designed to study Europa in detail.

- **March 1, 2025:** a gravity-assist flyby of Mars, about 884 km (550 miles) above the surface. Several instruments, including the ice-penetrating radar, were tested there.
- **November 2025:** its ultraviolet spectrograph observed the interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS from about one astronomical unit away, as a calibration exercise.
- **December 3, 2026:** a second gravity assist, passing about 3,200 km (2,000 miles) from Earth. The flyby also gives the magnetometer its only post-launch chance for an absolute calibration against Earth's well-known magnetic field.
- **April 2030:** arrival and orbit insertion at Jupiter, followed by **49 planned close flybys** of Europa, some as low as 25 km.
- **September 2034:** the current plan ends the mission by deliberately crashing the spacecraft into the moon Ganymede.

Clipper carries nine science instruments plus a gravity experiment that uses its radio. Among them: cameras, infrared and ultraviolet spectrometers, a thermal imager to hunt for warm spots, an ice-penetrating radar (REASON), a magnetometer meant to confirm the ocean and measure its depth and salinity, and a mass spectrometer (MASPEX) and dust analyzer to sample gas and particles above the surface.

## What it can and cannot tell us

NASA is explicit: **Europa Clipper is not a life-detection mission.** It has no instruments designed to find living things. Its goal is to work out whether Europa has the ingredients and conditions to support life: how thick the ice is, how the ocean exchanges material with the surface, what the ocean is made of, and whether the moon is active today. A "yes, habitable" answer would not mean anything lives there. It would mean Europa is a strong target for a future lander or deeper search.

## Our take

Europa is one of the best-supported candidates for a habitable environment beyond Earth: the ocean is very likely real, and carbon appears to be coming up from it. But no evidence of life on Europa exists, and the plume evidence that once promised easy access to the ocean has largely faded. The decisive measurements start when Clipper reaches Jupiter in 2030.
