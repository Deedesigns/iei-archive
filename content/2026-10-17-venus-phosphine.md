## The headline that launched a thousand papers

![Two side-by-side views of Venus as a creamy, cloud-covered globe; the right-hand version has enhanced contrast that brings out faint orange-brown cloud bands](venus-mariner10.jpg)
*Venus as Mariner 10 saw it on its 1974 flyby, newly reprocessed by JPL in 2020: approximate natural colour on the left, enhanced contrast on the right. The phosphine debate is about a faint trace gas in and above these sulphuric-acid clouds. Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech (PIA23791), via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:PIA23791-Venus-RealAndEnhancedContrastViews-20200608.jpg), public domain.*

On **September 14, 2020**, Jane Greaves (Cardiff University) and colleagues published "Phosphine gas in the cloud decks of Venus" in *Nature Astronomy*. They reported a faint absorption line at about **1.1 mm wavelength (267 GHz)**, the first rotational transition of **phosphine (PH₃)**, first in data from the **James Clerk Maxwell Telescope (JCMT)** in Hawaii taken in June 2017 and then in follow-up data from **ALMA** in Chile taken in March 2019. Their estimate was about **20 parts per billion (ppb)**.

Why the excitement? Phosphine is a molecule of one phosphorus and three hydrogen atoms. Venus's atmosphere is highly oxidizing, so phosphine shouldn't last there. On Earth, phosphine is associated with anaerobic microbes and with industry. The team modelled lightning, volcanoes, meteorites and other non-biological sources and found none that could make enough of it. That made phosphine a candidate **biosignature**, a sign that life might be making it. Venus's surface is about 450 °C and can't host life as we know it, but the cloud layer roughly 50–60 km up has Earth-like temperatures and pressures. Scientists have speculated about life there for decades.

## How the claim came under fire

Within weeks, the detection was being taken apart:

- **The ALMA calibration problem.** ALMA staff found an issue in the data used to confirm the detection. The data were recalibrated and reposted to the archive, and ALMA added a note to its original press release in November 2020.
- **Snellen et al. 2020** (*Astronomy & Astrophysics*) reanalysed the ALMA data and found **no statistically significant phosphine**. They argued the original processing method could produce false features.
- **Villanueva et al. 2021** (*Nature Astronomy*), a large team led by NASA Goddard, reanalysed both datasets. They pointed out that a **sulphur dioxide (SO₂) line sits only 1.3 km/s away** from the phosphine line, too close to separate at the resolution of the data. They set upper limits for phosphine above the clouds (above 75 km) that clashed with the original claim. SO₂ is abundant on Venus and its levels swing a lot over time.
- **Lincowski et al. 2021** (*Astrophysical Journal Letters*) modelled the atmosphere and argued that the JCMT feature is consistent with SO₂ in the upper atmosphere (the mesosphere) rather than phosphine in the clouds.
- **Infrared and spacecraft searches found nothing.** Ground-based infrared observations (Encrenaz et al. 2020) and solar-occultation spectra from ESA's **Venus Express** (Trompet et al. 2021) set tight upper limits above the cloud tops.
- **SOFIA, November 2021.** Cordiner et al. (2022, *Geophysical Research Letters*) flew NASA's airborne SOFIA observatory three times, targeting several phosphine lines near 533 and 1,067 GHz. They detected **no phosphine** and set a disk-averaged upper limit of **0.8 ppb at 75–110 km**.
- **More infrared limits, 2024.** Encrenaz et al. (2024, *A&A*), using data collected since 2012, found no phosphine anywhere on the disk and set a 3-sigma upper limit of **3 ppb** at the cloud tops.

## How the original team answered

The Greaves group never withdrew the claim, but they revised it. After reprocessing the corrected ALMA data, they reported phosphine at about **5-sigma** confidence. The planet-wide average dropped to **about 1–4 ppb**, with possible local peaks of 5–10 ppb. They argued that the JCMT line is too broad to be SO₂, and that the gap between ALMA and JCMT could be explained if phosphine varies by about a factor of ten over time. Using the same SOFIA data with a different reduction method, Greaves et al. (2023) got about **1 ppb**. They suggested sunlight destroys phosphine, so it might be near 20 ppb in the Venusian morning and about 1 ppb in the evening. Cordiner et al. (2023) disputed that reduction and stood by their upper limit.

They also pointed to old data from the probe that actually entered Venus's atmosphere. **Mogul et al. 2021** reanalysed mass spectra from NASA's **Pioneer Venus Large Probe** (1978) and argued for phosphine and other disequilibrium species at about 51 km. That's a re-reading of 40-year-old data from an instrument that wasn't designed to look for phosphine, and other teams have questioned how much those mass peaks can tell us.

## JCMT-Venus: the long game (2022–2026)

To settle it, the team started **JCMT-Venus**, a long-term monitoring programme on the JCMT. It uses a newer receiver (ʻŪʻū) with wider bandwidth, so it can watch phosphine, HDO (a stand-in for water), SO₂ and other molecules at the same time. Campaigns ran in February 2022, July 2023 and August–September 2023. Clements told reporters the first run alone gave about **140 times** as much data as the original detection.

- At the Royal Astronomical Society's **National Astronomy Meeting on July 17, 2024**, David Clements (Imperial College London) reported **new phosphine detections** that seemed to follow a **day–night pattern**, with less phosphine in sunlight. A separate team led by Greaves reported a **tentative detection of ammonia** with the Green Bank Telescope. These were conference talks, **not peer-reviewed papers**.
- A preliminary analysis in IAU proceedings put phosphine at about **0.3 parts per million near 55 km**, roughly matching the Pioneer Venus reanalysis. The authors called it preliminary and said "much more work remains."
- An abstract for the **EPSC 2026** meeting (Tang, Clements, Greaves, Richards and Peel) says **four campaigns** are done, with a fifth approved for July 2026. It promises a talk on "the re-detection of PH₃" and its robustness. It also stresses that standing-wave ripples in the spectra are "a common issue in Venus mm/submm spectroscopy". Those ripples are the same kind of instrument artefact that critics blamed for the original signal.

As of today we haven't found a **peer-reviewed paper** that independently confirms phosphine on Venus with a different telescope and a different analysis team. The newer detections come from the original group, and the strongest non-detections come from independent groups using different instruments and wavelengths.

## Why both sides can partly be right

The measurements don't all probe the **same altitude, time or place**. JCMT and ALMA look at a millimetre line formed roughly 50–110 km up. Infrared limits sample the cloud tops. SOFIA looked at 75–110 km. If phosphine really is destroyed by sunlight, or confined to deeper cloud layers, a detection at one altitude and a non-detection at another wouldn't strictly contradict each other. That loophole is real. It also makes the hypothesis harder to test, which is why independent replication matters so much.

## Our verdict: Disputed

**What is established:** a faint feature near the phosphine frequency appears in some reductions of some millimetre data. Several independent searches at other wavelengths and altitudes found no phosphine, down to below 1 ppb in places. The original abundance claim of about 20 ppb has been revised down a lot.

**What is claimed:** that the feature is phosphine, that it varies with time of day and altitude, and that ammonia may be there too. The newest supporting results come mostly from conference presentations by the original team.

**What is speculation:** that the phosphine, if it exists, is made by life. Even supporters say unknown chemistry is a live alternative. The researchers who proposed volcanic phosphine production are one example.

So it's **Disputed**: not debunked, because some data still show a signal and loopholes remain, but not confirmed either. The case is a textbook lesson in how hard remote biosignature detection is. It's the same problem we covered for [K2-18b](https://deedesigns.github.io/iei-archive/articles/k2-18b-biosignature-claim.html), at much closer range.

## What would settle it

Direct sampling. NASA's **DAVINCI** entry probe, which carries instruments that can measure trace gases as it falls through the clouds, survived 2026 budget fights and is working toward a launch in the early 2030s. NASA's **VERITAS** radar orbiter has slipped to no earlier than 2031. ESA's **EnVision** has moved to 2032 while Europe builds its own radar to replace the one NASA had promised. The privately funded **Rocket Lab–MIT** probe, built to study cloud particles for organic material, is still waiting on Rocket Lab's Neutron rocket and has no confirmed launch date. Until something actually sniffs the clouds, Venus's phosphine will stay an argument rather than an answer. This page reflects sources we could verify as of October 17, 2026.
