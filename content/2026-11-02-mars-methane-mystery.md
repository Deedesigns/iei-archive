## Why one gas gets so much attention

On Earth, living things make more than 90% of the methane in the atmosphere; the rest comes from geology. That's how Michael Mumma's team put it in their 2009 *Science* paper, and it's why methane on Mars is such a big deal. Sunlight should break methane down in a few centuries on Mars, so if it's in the air today, something has been releasing it recently. That something could be microbes underground. It could just as easily be water reacting with rock (a process called serpentinization), gas leaking from old ice, or something we haven't thought of.

So methane isn't proof of life. But it's one of the few signals on Mars that would point to something active happening right now.

## A history of false starts

Mars methane has fooled people before. In 1969, just two days after the Mariner 7 flyby, the spacecraft's infrared team announced methane (and ammonia) at the edge of the south polar cap. They soon realized the absorption bands could be explained by carbon dioxide ice. Kevin Zahnle, Richard Freedman and David Catling retold the story in a skeptical 2011 review, calling it a lesson that "spectral detection of methane on Mars can trick even experienced researchers."

The modern claims started in 2004:

- **Mars Express (ESA), 2004.** Vittorio Formisano's team used the orbiter's Planetary Fourier Spectrometer and reported a global average of **10 ± 5 parts per billion by volume (ppbv)**, varying from 0 to 30 ppbv across the planet.
- **Telescopes on Earth, 2009.** Mumma and colleagues reported methane plumes in northern summer 2003, with the main plume holding about **19,000 metric tons** of methane.
- **The pushback, 2011.** Zahnle, Freedman and Catling argued that methane that comes and goes this quickly is physically and chemically implausible. They also showed that the Earth-based detections sat right on top of methane lines from Earth's own atmosphere, which must be modeled out. For the cleanest set of lines, they found the data fit an upper limit of about 3 ppbv.

## Curiosity on the ground

NASA sent the Curiosity rover to Gale Crater in 2012 with a methane sniffer on board: the Tunable Laser Spectrometer, part of its Sample Analysis at Mars (SAM) lab. Its first results found no methane. Then came the surprises.

- **The 2013-14 spike.** Over four measurements in late 2013 and early 2014, Curiosity saw methane at about 7 ppbv. A later reanalysis put the average at **7.6 ± 1.6 ppbv** (Webster et al. 2018).
- **The seasonal background.** Using a more sensitive "enrichment" mode, Chris Webster's team reported in *Science* in 2018 that Gale Crater has a low background of **0.41 ± 0.16 ppbv**, rising and falling with the seasons between **0.24 and 0.65 ppbv** and peaking near the end of northern summer. They said this pattern was consistent with "small localized sources of methane released from martian surface or subsurface reservoirs."
- **The June 2019 record.** Curiosity measured about **21 ppbv**, the most it had ever found. A follow-up run that weekend found less than 1 ppbv, so it was another short-lived plume. (A later paper by Moores and colleagues gives the value as 19 ppbv.)

## An independent confirmation, once

The strongest piece of evidence that Curiosity isn't just measuring its own exhaust came in 2019. Marco Giuranna's team reanalyzed Mars Express data and reported **15.5 ± 2.5 ppbv** of methane above Gale Crater on **16 June 2013**, one day after Curiosity recorded a spike. Mars Express didn't see methane on its other passes. The team traced a likely source region east of Gale, where faults might reach into buried ice and release trapped gas now and then. That's the only time two different spacecraft have caught the same methane event.

## The orbiter that sees nothing

In 2016 Europe and Russia launched the ExoMars **Trace Gas Orbiter (TGO)**, designed specifically to find trace gases like methane. Its instruments look through the atmosphere at sunrise and sunset, which makes them very sensitive.

- **2019:** Oleg Korablev and colleagues reported in *Nature* that TGO found **no methane** from April to August 2018, with an upper limit of about **0.05 ppbv**, which they said is 10 to 100 times lower than earlier detections.
- **2021:** Franck Montmessin and colleagues added 1.44 Mars years of data and still found nothing. They set an upper limit of **20 parts per trillion** (0.02 ppbv), about ten times below the lowest amount Curiosity has reported.

Here's the problem. On Mars, methane should last for centuries and spread around the whole planet within a few months. If Gale Crater were steadily leaking enough to keep its air at 0.4 ppbv, Korablev's team calculated, the global level would pass TGO's detection limit within a few decades. So either something removes methane very quickly, or one of the measurements is wrong.

## A possible way out: methane that only shows up at night

The leading idea is about timing. Curiosity's sensitive measurements were mostly taken at night, while TGO looks during sunrise and sunset, and from a few kilometers up. A 2021 study by Webster and colleagues in *Astronomy & Astrophysics* reported that Curiosity's daytime measurements fell below its detection limit while nighttime ones didn't. That fits a picture where methane seeps out slowly, collects near the ground in the calm night air inside the crater, and then gets mixed into the much bigger daytime atmosphere and diluted.

That explains why the two spacecraft could both be right about Gale. It doesn't explain the bigger puzzle: even if the gas gets diluted, it should still build up globally unless something destroys it much faster than known chemistry allows. As Zahnle's team pointed out in 2011, any fast chemical destruction would need a matching unknown source of oxidizing power. That part is still unsolved.

## What's evidence, what's claim, what's speculation

- **Evidence:** Curiosity's instrument has repeatedly measured small methane signals in Gale Crater with a repeatable seasonal pattern, plus several spikes. Mars Express independently caught one spike above Gale in June 2013. TGO, with better sensitivity, has found no methane anywhere it has looked.
- **Claim:** That Mars has real, active methane sources. Many Curiosity team members support this, while the TGO results and earlier skeptics push back. The early Mars Express and Earth-telescope reports of 10 ppbv or more are now widely doubted, since TGO would have easily seen that much.
- **Speculation:** That the methane comes from microbes. None of the instruments can tell where it comes from. Geological sources like water-rock reactions or old trapped gas in ice fit the data just as well.

## Verdict

**Disputed.** Here the dispute is between the data, not between believers and skeptics. Curiosity's readings are real measurements from a careful team, and one spike was independently confirmed. But the orbiter built to settle the question sees nothing, and the explanation that would let both be right, methane trapped near the ground at night, still needs a way to destroy methane quickly that hasn't been found. Even if Martian methane is real, it doesn't point to life over geology. What would settle it? Measuring the isotopes in the methane (the carbon-13 ratio could hint at biological vs. geological origin), or a methane detector parked on the surface running day and night next to the source region Giuranna's team suggested east of Gale.
