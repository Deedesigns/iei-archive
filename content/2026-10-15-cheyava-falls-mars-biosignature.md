## The rock with leopard spots

![Close-up of a reddish Martian rock between white mineral veins, covered in small pale spots with dark rims; one spot and a green olivine crystal are circled and labelled](cheyava-falls-leopard-spots.jpg)
*"Cheyava Falls", imaged by the WATSON camera on Perseverance's robotic arm on July 18, 2024 (sol 1,212). The annotated version marks one of the millimetre-size "leopard spots" and a grain of olivine. Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech/MSSS, via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:1-PIA26368-Perseverance_Finds_a_Rock_with_Leopard_Spots-annotated.png) (PIA26368), public domain.*

In July 2024 NASA's Perseverance rover reached the **"Bright Angel" formation**, a set of light-toned outcrops along **Neretva Vallis**, an ancient river valley about 400 metres wide that once carried water into Jezero Crater. There it found an arrowhead-shaped rock about 1 metre by 0.6 metres, nicknamed **"Cheyava Falls"** after a Grand Canyon waterfall.

The rock is a mudstone: fine clay and silt that settled out of water. Its reddish bands are speckled with pale spots, each ringed by a thin dark rim, that the team nicknamed "leopard spots", plus even smaller dark specks they called "poppy seeds". On July 21, 2024, the rover drilled a core from the rock, named **"Sapphire Canyon"**, and sealed it in a sample tube.

## What the 2025 study found

On **September 10, 2025**, the science team, led by Joel Hurowitz of Stony Brook University, published its analysis in *Nature*, and NASA announced that the sample contains a **"potential biosignature"**. NASA defines that as "a substance or structure that might have a biological origin but requires more data or further study before a conclusion can be reached about the absence or presence of life."

The paper reports:

- **Organic carbon** in the Bright Angel mudstones, detected by the rover's SHERLOC Raman instrument.
- **Poppy seeds** about 0.1 to 0.2 mm across and **leopard spots** about 0.2 to 1 mm across, which formed in place after the mud was deposited rather than being washed in as grains.
- The rims and nodules are rich in iron and phosphorus, consistent with **vivianite** (an iron phosphate). The spot cores contain an iron sulfide consistent with **greigite**.
- The minerals appear to have formed through chemical reactions in which organic matter gave up electrons to iron and sulfate, and the geology points to **low temperatures**.

On Earth, vivianite commonly forms in lake and sea sediments as a by-product of microbes that "breathe" iron, and greigite can be produced by sulfate-reducing microbes. Microbes run exactly these reactions to get energy. That is why the pairing caught the team's attention.

## Why it is not proof

The paper itself does not claim life. It tests a "null hypothesis" that the minerals formed without biology, and reports where that explanation runs into trouble:

- **Vivianite is the easier part.** Many organic compounds can chemically reduce iron minerals at 10 to 80 °C without life. Whether the specific organics in this rock could do it is untested. Hurowitz told The Planetary Society he expects someone may well show vivianite can form this way abiotically.
- **Greigite is the harder part.** Turning sulfate into sulfide without microbes is extremely slow below roughly 150 to 200 °C, according to the paper. The rocks show no evidence of being heated, and reaching such temperatures by burial would require more than about 5 km of overlying rock.
- **Context matters.** Similar bleached "reduction spots" in rocks on Earth are sometimes credited to microbes, but the paper notes that is "not a universally held perspective", and at least one study has made such spots by an inorganic process.

Perseverance's project scientist, Katie Stack Morgan, summed it up in NASA's announcement: abiotic explanations "are less likely given the paper's findings," but "we cannot rule them out."

## The 2026 organics paper

On **June 24, 2026**, a second team led by Ashley Murphy (Planetary Science Institute) and Kyle Uckert (NASA JPL) published a SHERLOC study in *Science Advances*. From 18 spectral maps (1,800 spectra) across Bright Angel targets, they report hundreds of organic detections in two mudstones, which they call "the most robust organic detection in Jezero crater thus far." The material is **macromolecular carbon**: large, tangled networks of carbon atoms of the kind found in Earth rocks and in meteorites.

The authors are explicit that rover Raman data "cannot determine whether these organics denote abiotic or biotic sources." The finding strengthens the case that organics really are present and widespread in these rocks. It does not tell us whether anything ever lived in them.

## The sample is stuck on Mars

The rover was designed to scout and store samples, not to settle this kind of question. Settling it would take laboratory instruments on Earth, for example ones that can identify exactly which organic molecules are present and check whether the chemistry matches digestion by microbes.

That is the problem. The NASA–ESA **Mars Sample Return** campaign, meant to fetch the tubes, saw its estimated cost grow to as much as $11 billion with return dates slipping toward 2040. NASA's fiscal 2026 budget request proposed ending it, and in **January 2026** Congress passed a spending bill that rejected most proposed NASA science cuts but did not fund the existing program. Instead it set aside $110 million for a "Mars Future Missions" line to develop technology that could support later robotic or crewed missions.

As of mid-October 2026, NASA has announced no replacement plan or return date. Administrator Jared Isaacman said on August 30, 2026 that "there's no question we're going to go get samples," but that whether robots or astronauts do it, and when, remained open. Lockheed Martin has pitched a fixed-price concept of under $3 billion to return 30 of the rover's samples by 2037. Meanwhile China's **Tianwen-3** mission, expected to launch in 2028, aims to bring its own Martian samples to Earth around 2031, though Jezero Crater does not appear on its candidate landing-site list.

## Our verdict: Unexplained

The leopard spots are real, the organic carbon is real, and the mineral pattern is the kind microbes leave behind on Earth. No one has yet shown a non-biological process that makes all of it at low temperature. But "we haven't found another explanation yet" is not evidence of life, and Mars has fooled scientists before: the 1996 claim of microbial fossils in the Martian meteorite ALH84001 was eventually undercut by non-biological explanations. NASA's then-acting administrator, Sean Duffy, called the Cheyava Falls finding "the closest we have ever come to discovering life on Mars", and that is a fair description of a strong lead. It stays unexplained until the Sapphire Canyon core can be examined on Earth. This page reflects published work we could verify as of October 15, 2026.
