## The press release that started it all

![Top of the Roswell Daily Record front page for July 8, 1947, with the banner headline "RAAF Captures Flying Saucer On Ranch in Roswell Region"](roswell-daily-record.jpg)
*Top of the front page of the* Roswell Daily Record, *July 8, 1947. Credit: Roswell Daily Record, via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Roswell_Daily_Record._July_8,_1947._RAAF_Captures_Flying_Saucer_On_Ranch_in_Roswell_Region._Top_of_front_page.jpg), public domain.*

On **July 8, 1947**, the public information office at **Roswell Army Air Field (RAAF)**, home of the 509th Bomb Group, announced that it had recovered a "flying disc." The *Roswell Daily Record* ran the banner headline **"RAAF Captures Flying Saucer On Ranch In Roswell Region."** Its story said the group's intelligence officer, **Major Jesse A. Marcel**, had recovered the object from a ranch and that it had been "flown to higher headquarters."

Within a day the story flipped. **Brigadier General Roger Ramey**, commander of the Eighth Air Force in Fort Worth, said the debris was a weather balloon. The July 9 paper ran "Ramey Empties Roswell Saucer." The timing matters. The national "flying saucer" craze had started only two weeks earlier with Kenneth Arnold's June 24 sighting, and newspapers everywhere were chasing disc reports.

## What the rancher actually found

The July 9 *Roswell Daily Record* interview with the rancher, **W. W. "Mac" Brazel**, is the earliest detailed description of the debris, and it is quoted at length in the 1994 Air Force report. Brazel said he and his son found "a large area of bright wreckage made up of rubber strips, tinfoil, a rather tough paper, and sticks" on **June 14, 1947**. He gathered some of it on July 4, heard about flying discs, and went to the sheriff in Roswell on **July 7**.

His description is modest. The rubber was "smoky gray" and scattered over about **200 yards**. Gathered up, the foil, paper, tape and sticks made a bundle about **three feet long and 7 or 8 inches thick**. He guessed the whole lot weighed **maybe five pounds**. There was "no sign of any metal" for an engine and no propellers. He also noted "considerable scotch tape and some tape with flowers printed upon it."

*Major Jesse A. Marcel with debris from the Roswell find at Fort Worth Army Air Field, July 8, 1947. Credit: Fort Worth Star-Telegram photo, University of Texas at Arlington Libraries, via [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Marcel-roswell-debris_0.jpg), public domain.*

Photographers from the *Fort Worth Star-Telegram* shot Ramey and Marcel with the wreckage. The 1994 Air Force review compared first-generation prints and negatives from UT Arlington and found that the **same debris** appears in the Marcel and Ramey photos. That undercuts the later claim that "real" saucer debris was swapped out for balloon parts. An **FBI Dallas teletype** of July 8, 1947, often cited by cover-up proponents, describes the object as resembling "a high altitude weather balloon with a radar reflector."

## The silence, and the revival

For about three decades, almost nobody treated Roswell as a UFO case. The Air Force's own UFO projects (Sign, Grudge, and Blue Book, 1948–1969) do not mention it. That changed in **1978**, when a tabloid reported Marcel's claim that he had recovered UFO debris, and UFO researcher **Stanton Friedman** began investigating his story. In **1980**, William Moore and Charles Berlitz published *The Roswell Incident*. Later books by Kevin Randle and Donald Schmitt, and Friedman and Don Berliner, added second crash sites, exotic metals with "hieroglyphics," and recovered alien bodies.

The 1994 Air Force report notes a key weakness: almost all of this testimony was **gathered 30 to 45 years after the event**, often second- or third-hand. The authors also did not agree with one another on dates, locations, or how many bodies there were.

## What the government investigations found

In **1993–94**, New Mexico Congressman **Steven Schiff** asked the General Accounting Office (GAO) to find any records of the crash. That led to three reports.

- **The Air Force's 1994 report** (published in 1995 as *The Roswell Report: Fact vs. Fiction in the New Mexico Desert*). Its records search found no spike in operations or security at Roswell in July 1947. The 509th's own July history says the "flying disc" "turned out to be a radar tracking balloon." The Air Force concluded the debris most likely came from **Project Mogul**, a then-TOP SECRET program run with New York University to detect Soviet nuclear tests with balloon-borne acoustic sensors. Project engineer **Charles B. Moore** said the material most likely came from **NYU Flight 4**. That was an unlogged "service flight" launched on **June 4, 1947**, and never recovered. It carried neoprene balloons and radar reflectors made of foil-backed paper, balsa sticks, and tape. Some of the early reflectors used purplish-pink tape with flower symbols.
- **The GAO's July 1995 report (GAO/NSIAD-95-187).** It found four military air accidents reported in New Mexico in July 1947, all after July 8, and none matching Roswell. It also found that RAAF administrative records from **March 1945 to December 1949** and outgoing messages from **October 1946 to December 1949** had been **destroyed**, without a proper note of who authorized it. The archivist said that was common for Air Force records of the period. The GAO took no position on what the debris was.
- **The Air Force's 1997 report, *The Roswell Report: Case Closed*.** This one dealt with the alien-body stories. It concluded the "aliens" were most likely **anthropomorphic test dummies** dropped from high-altitude balloons in the 1950s. It said the "bodies" at the base hospital were probably a mix-up of a **1956 KC-97 crash** that killed 11 airmen and a **1959 manned-balloon mishap** that injured two pilots. Critics point out that those events happened years after 1947. The Air Force's argument is that witnesses merged separate memories into one story.

In **March 2024**, the Pentagon's **All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO)** reviewed this history in its *Historical Record Report, Volume I*. It found no evidence of a UFO event, a cover-up, or recovered aliens or extraterrestrial material.

## Claims vs. evidence at a glance

- **Claim: The Army captured a flying disc.** *Evidence:* The army did say that on July 8, 1947. The physical descriptions from that week (foil, rubber, sticks, tape, about five pounds) match a balloon train with radar targets, not a vehicle.
- **Claim: The debris was switched before the Fort Worth photos.** *Evidence:* The first-generation negatives show the same material with both Marcel and Ramey. Sheridan Cavitt, the counter-intelligence officer who went to the ranch with Marcel, said in a 1994 sworn statement that the photos matched what he picked up.
- **Claim: Alien bodies were recovered.** *Evidence:* No 1947 document or photo supports this. The stories first appear after 1978, from memories decades old.
- **Claim: Records were destroyed to hide the truth.** *Evidence:* Some RAAF records really were destroyed without proper paperwork (GAO, 1995). The GAO and the National Archives' records center described this as a common, sloppy practice of the era, not something targeted.
- **Claim: The government lied in 1947.** *Evidence:* Partly true, in a mundane way. "Weather balloon" hid the classified purpose of Project Mogul. The hardware itself was unclassified.

## Our verdict: Explained

Roswell is the founding myth of modern UFO lore, and part of it is true: the military gave the public a misleading story in 1947. But the secret was a Cold War spy balloon, not a spaceship. Every account from that week describes lightweight foil, rubber, sticks and tape that match Project Mogul hardware. The crashed-saucer and alien-body versions only appeared 30 or more years later and conflict with one another. Three federal reviews (Air Force 1994/1997, GAO 1995, AARO 2024) found no supporting records. One honest caveat is that the destroyed RAAF records and the unlogged Mogul "service flights" mean the exact flight can't be proven beyond doubt. That is a gap in the paperwork, not evidence of aliens.
